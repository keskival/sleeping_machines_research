\sect{The ambition}{One substrate for machine intelligence}

Today's AI runs on a clock: every input is chopped into fixed steps, and the whole model works at every step whether or not anything happened. The real world is not like that. Purchases, heartbeats, sensor readings, messages, machine logs and words arrive as \emph{events}, irregularly, with meaning in their timing and in their silences.

Sleeping Machines builds learning machines in which \textbf{time itself does part of the computation}. Our goal is one trainable substrate that spans:

\textbf{What makes it different, in plain terms:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Memories that live through silence.} Each memory keeps evolving between events, so ``how long since'' and ``what happened in between'' are information, not missing data.
\item \textbf{A race of clocks decides what happens next.} Possible next events race against each other; the one that fires first wins. This gives exact predictions of both \emph{when} and \emph{what}, and the same mathematics reproduces the attention mechanism inside today's large language models.
\item \textbf{Only the relevant parts wake up.} Updates are sparse and addressed: the machine can hold far more capacity than it uses for any single event, which is where the name comes from.
\item \textbf{Learning from roads not taken.} The model is also credited for the alternatives that lost the race, so it learns which routes to take.
\end{itemize}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{Why this matters commercially.} Most of the world's valuable data is timestamped events: transactions, clinical records, telemetry, security and IT logs, user behaviour. Models that understand time natively can be more accurate on that data and much cheaper to run, on CPUs and edge devices as well as in datacentres. The same principles point to hardware without a global clock, where idle capacity costs almost nothing.
\end{callout}

\sect{The proving ground}{The benchmarks, in plain language}

We compete on established public benchmarks, using their official data splits and scoring rules, against the best results other teams have published. The headline event benchmark is \textbf{EasyTPP} (ICLR 2024), the standard test suite for predicting \emph{when} the next event happens and \emph{what} it will be. Its current leader is \textbf{S2P2} (NeurIPS 2025, GE HealthCare and UC Irvine), a modern state-space model. The \textbf{Temporal Graph Benchmark} (TGB, NeurIPS 2023) tests who interacts with whom, and when, against temporal graph neural networks.

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{tile}
\textbf{Reading the scores.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Log-likelihood (nats per event), higher is better.} It measures how much probability the model gave to what actually happened, both its timing and its type. A gain of 0.08 nats per event means the model assigns about 8\% more probability to every real event, on average, compounding over every event in a sequence. Values can be negative; less negative is better.
\item \textbf{AUPRC, higher is better (0 to 1).} How well the model ranks the rare patients who develop sepsis (4\% of stays) ahead of everyone else, with emphasis on raising true alarms without false ones. It is the clinically relevant metric when the condition is rare. \textbf{AUROC} (0 to 1, higher is better) measures overall ranking.
\item \textbf{Negative log-likelihood per sequence, lower is better.} The same probability measure summed over a whole sequence and negated, as the neural-TPP benchmark reports it; time and type (``mark'') parts are reported separately and add up to the total.
\item \textbf{NDCG@10 and MRR, higher is better (0 to 1).} How well the model ranks the true next partners or pages near the top of a list of all candidates.
\item \textbf{Size and cost, lower is better.} Parameters measure how big a model is; multiply-adds per event measure the work needed for each prediction, which drives running cost and energy.
\end{itemize}
\end{tile}

\sect{Rigor}{How we know the results are real}

\begin{minipage}[t]{.485\linewidth}
\begin{tile}[height=31mm]
\textbf{\faLock\ Sealed tests, rules fixed in advance.} Every win uses the benchmark's official data splits. The reporting rule is written down before the test set is touched, and each model is scored on the test set exactly once.
\end{tile}
\begin{tile}[height=31mm]
\textbf{\faRedo\ Independent runs.} Benchmark headline results are averages of independent runs (five seeds or official splits; three pre-registered seeds on TGB), with its spread: 40 of 41 runs are ahead of the best published result.
\end{tile}
\begin{tile}[height=31mm]
\textbf{\faServer\ Reproduced on separate hardware.} The Taxi win was reproduced independently on a separate desktop computer with separately downloaded data: 0.5252 vs 0.5250 nats per event \up, every run above S2P2. One-command kits let third parties rerun the EasyTPP and both TGB wins.
\end{tile}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{.485\linewidth}
\begin{tile}[height=31mm]
\textbf{\faCheckDouble\ The benchmark's own scorer agrees.} Re-scoring our models with EasyTPP's own estimation method gives the same results within its sampling noise.
\end{tile}
\begin{tile}[height=31mm]
\textbf{\faSearch\ We found and closed a scoring pitfall.} Timestamps in these datasets are rounded (for example to whole seconds). Flexible models can exploit the rounding for large, fake gains. We built an audit that removes the rounding; we withdrew our own early results that failed it, and every reported win passes it.
\end{tile}
\begin{tile}[height=31mm]
\textbf{\faBalanceScale\ Losses reported as plainly as wins.} Where we are behind, we say so with the numbers and the next test (next page). Comparisons always use the same units and protocol as the published result.
\end{tile}

\sect{Learning more economically}{Teaching the model how to learn}

The earlier pages show that our models can make strong predictions with small
models. We are also tackling the cost of \emph{learning}: deciding which parts
of a model should change when new information arrives.

A sparse model takes only a few routes through its available memory and
computation. To improve, it needs to learn from those choices, including
alternatives it did not take. Trying every alternative can make training
expensive even when prediction is cheap.

\begin{callout}
\textbf{Our approach.} Use what the model discovers while making predictions
to help it evaluate alternatives and choose better learning updates. The
ambition is a model that learns both \emph{what to predict} and \emph{how to
improve}, using the same event-based architecture.
\end{callout}

\vspace{3mm}
\begin{minipage}[t]{.32\linewidth}\stat{98.4\%}{less error when estimating the value of alternative learning routes}\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{.32\linewidth}\stat{2.09$\times$}{faster evaluation of alternatives in the measured experiment}\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{.32\linewidth}\stat{77.8\%}{less randomness in the route controller's learning signal}\end{minipage}

\vspace{3mm}
\textbf{What these results mean.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{More accurate guidance.} On eight new language samples, information
from the model and its learning state estimated the relative value of alternative
routes more accurately than a fixed estimate. Every sample improved.
\item \textbf{Less repeated work.} Reusing this information, with an exact
correction, took 12.9 seconds instead of 26.9 seconds for separately evaluating
all alternatives. Both measurements include discovering the alternatives.
\item \textbf{A steadier learning signal.} Random route selection introduces noise
into learning. In a separate controller experiment, the corrected signal reduced
that source of noise while preserving its average direction.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Significance.} These experiments demonstrate that information already
produced by a model can make an expensive part of learning more accurate and
more economical. They give a concrete engineering basis for our learning
architecture. The measured speedup applies to evaluating learning alternatives,
not to training an entire model.

\textbf{Learning rules can themselves improve.} In a separate event-prediction
experiment, a trained controller adjusted the model's learning updates and
improved prediction quality in all nine tested combinations of model depth and
initialization. Those fits used more CPU time. This shows that learned update
rules can improve quality within our model family.

{\footnotesize\color{muted}Status: a working research prototype. The 98.4\% result
uses eight private language samples; speed uses four samples and three timing
repeats; noise uses four samples and a fixed controller. These are separate
component measurements. The update-controller result uses three initializations
at depths 2/4/8 on Taxi training/development data and retains ordinary error
propagation. Detailed methods and completed result hashes are in
\texttt{investment/forward\_credit\_evidence\_20261011.json}.}

\sect{Industrial fault detection}{A sealed win on interleaved factory logs}

A factory log interleaves the events of many items and machines with no item
labels. A failing machine shows up as steps that slowly take longer. Our model
learns, from normal logs alone, how long each production step takes, links each
event to the earlier event it most likely continues, and keeps a separate running
anomaly score for every step. \textbf{A slowing machine stands out within the first
half of a production run.}

\midrule

\textbf{Why this matters.} Predictive maintenance runs on exactly this kind of data:

\textbf{How the test was run.} The test data were generated by our open simulator only

\sect{What the evidence adds up to}{A proven prediction engine and a developing learning engine}

Sleeping Machines aims to be a general-purpose substrate for language and
reasoning, multimodal world models, embodiment, continual learning,
communication, self-design and hardware. Its evidence already spans several
parts of that ambition.

\begin{tile}
\textbf{Strong prediction with small models.} The public benchmark results lead
this brief because they are the strongest evidence: nine benchmark wins across
four kinds of data, including all five datasets of the standard event benchmark,
with models up to 15 times smaller. Two preregistered confirmations add a new
month of market forecasting while every model learns, and a sealed industrial
fault-detection test: 0.801 versus 0.704 AUROC for the best Transformer. That gives the model family a measured
advantage on specific tasks, using existing computers.
\end{tile}

\begin{tile}
\textbf{A practical route to improving learning.} We have measured more accurate
and faster evaluation of learning alternatives, better predictions from learned
update rules, and online market learning that stays ahead of LSTM and Transformer
learners on unseen data. Together they show working pieces of a machine that learns how
to learn. Each result has its own scope; the pieces are not one combined speedup.
\end{tile}

\begin{tile}
\textbf{A mathematical foundation.} We have derived and numerically checked how
route-level learning errors depend on prediction uncertainty and differences
between alternatives. The direct route formulation can avoid multiplying local
estimation errors through successive layers. This explains why the design is
worth pursuing; it is an analytical result, separate from measured scaling.
\end{tile}

\vspace{3mm}
\textbf{Where we are behind.} On P12 mortality the family scores 0.871 versus 0.880 AUROC.
On the second event benchmark (page 5), our current model with addressed episodic memory is in development; its verdicts follow when the test splits are scored.

\textbf{Where scaling stands.} The wins above come from small models on event data. Large-scale
language is not yet won: our strongest token model scores 5.60 nats per token after 4 million
training tokens, and a new addressed episodic memory improves the earlier flagship by 0.35 nats,
but added depth does not yet pay for itself. A two-seed depth ladder is running now; the next
decisive steps are deeper models that learn their new layers and faster execution than the
current single-thread CPU training.

\begin{callout}
\textbf{The commercial significance.} The public wins support the value of small,
accurate models for timestamped data in industry, IT operations, security and
finance; the sealed fault-detection win and the new-month market win show it on
predictive maintenance and live market data. The learning work addresses a further source of cost: keeping models
useful as new information arrives. If the measured component advantages carry
through to complete applications, they could make continual adaptation more
affordable on local machines and support sparse, clockless learning hardware.
That broader opportunity is the significance of the design, rather than a
measured revenue, energy or whole-model training claim.
\end{callout}
